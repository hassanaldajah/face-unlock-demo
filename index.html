    How it works

    1. EnrollSave your face once. It stays in this browser.
    2. MatchLook at the camera until your face is recognised.
    3. Prove you are liveFollow two random prompts, like blink or turn your head.
    4. Fist & slideOpen your hand over the handle, make a fist to grab it, slide right, then let go. Hold a fist to lock again.

    Educational demo. Camera-based face matching can be fooled by photos and is not suitable for real security.

    Try to fool it

    Red team: three attacks you can run yourself.
    1. Photo attack. Turn the switch below off, then hold a photo of yourself (on your phone) up to the camera. Face matching alone lets it in.
    2. Same photo, liveness on. Turn the switch back on: the photo cannot blink, turn or open its mouth on command, so it fails.
    3. Brute force. Fail 5 times in a minute (someone else's face, or the photo) and watch the lockout and the alert in the log.

    How it defends itself

    Blue team: the protections in place.
    • Random liveness challenge. 2 of: blink, turn left, turn right, open mouth. The idea behind presentation attack detection (ISO/IEC 30107).
    • Continuous identity. The same face must stay in view during the challenge; swapping faces fails it.
    • Rate limiting. 5 failed attempts in 60 s lock the face step for 30 s, then 2 min, 8 min, up to 15 min. NIST SP 800-63B also caps failed biometric attempts.
    • Protected enrollment. Re-enrolling is only possible after unlocking, so nobody can swap in their own face.
    • Encryption at rest. The vault is AES-256-GCM encrypted with a key derived from your secret; bypassing the lock only reveals ciphertext.
    • Audit trail. Every attempt is logged below and can be exported as ECS-style JSON for a SIEM.
